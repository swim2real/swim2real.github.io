Swim2Real: Vision-Language Models for Simulator Calibration of a Soft Robotic Fish

Kevin Qiu1, Kyle Liam Walker2,3, Mike Yan Michelis4, Marek Cygan1, Josie Hughes2

1University of Warsaw 2EPFL 3University of Liverpool 4ETH Zurich

IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, 2026

Paper (PDF) arXiv 2603.20827 Code BibTeX

Swim2Real calibrates the simulator of a soft robotic fish from videos of the robot swimming. A vision-language model compares the real and simulated motion, diagnoses the mismatch, and proposes which of 16 fluid, motor and joint parameters to change. A backtracking line search keeps a step only if it lowers the error between simulated and tracked marker positions.

The model's proposals point in useful directions. We compared them with random directions and with the model's own proposals scrambled across parameters, under the same line search and simulator budget. In an independent, preregistered fifty-seed replication, they lowered the mean best-so-far marker error by 63.4 mm and 53.2 mm against these two controls (both p < 10−5, conditional on run completion). A second vision-language model shows the same effect.

Abstract

Calibrating a soft aquatic robot simulator requires matching coupled deformation and propulsion from limited pool recordings. Swim2Real uses a vision-language model (VLM) to compare real and simulated swimming videos and propose updates to 16 simulator parameters. A backtracking line search tests each direction and accepts updates that reduce marker error. To test whether the proposals carry useful directions, we compare them with random and signed-permuted directions under the same line search and simulator budget. In an independent, preregistered fifty-seed replication, Swim2Real reduces the mean best-so-far error over 40 simulator evaluations by 63.4 mm against random directions and 53.2 mm against permuted directions. Both paired comparisons pass Holm correction (p < 10−5), conditional on run completion. A 24-seed experiment with a second VLM supports the same direction effect. Backtracking averages 16.1 VLM proposals per run versus 39 for Vid2Sid, which attains lower error at the same simulator budget. Ten hardware trials demonstrate open-loop execution of simulator-generated motor commands.

How it works

A simulator can match one part of a swimming motion while missing another: the tail may bend with the wrong amplitude, or the body may advance too slowly. A scalar trajectory error measures the mismatch but does not say which parameters to change. Swim2Real asks a vision-language model to read the mismatch from video.

The calibration data are overhead recordings of the robot swimming at eight tail-beat frequencies, with nine markers tracked along its centerline.

Each round, Gemini 2.5 Pro receives overlay videos of the simulated and real centerlines at each fitted frequency and the real video at the worst-matching one. It also receives the marker errors, the swimming speeds, the current parameters with their bounds, and the outcomes of its earlier proposals. It returns a physical diagnosis and new values for the parameters it wants to change.

Swim2Real treats each proposal as a direction rather than an answer. A backtracking line search tries the full step, then the half and quarter steps, and keeps the first one that lowers marker error. If none does, the proposal is rejected and the model is told so in its next prompt. In a fifteen-seed study, 46 of the 97 accepted updates (47%) came from a half or quarter step, after the full step had not improved on the best error.

The 16 parameters are five fluid coefficients, the motor arm length, and the stiffness and damping of each of the tail's five joints. They are searched jointly rather than in hand-designed stages. Each run has a budget of 40 simulator evaluations and uses 16.1 VLM proposals on average. An evaluation of all eight recorded frequencies takes about 13 s on one CPU core.

VLM directions beat matched controls

Two matched controls keep the line search, the step sizes and the 40-evaluation budget, and change only the direction. Random directions use no model. Permuted directions query the VLM in the same way, then shuffle which parameters receive the proposed changes and randomize their signs. The step keeps its size but no longer targets the parameters the model named.

The preregistered replication ran all three methods from fifty fresh initializations and compared AUC40, the mean best-so-far error over the 40 evaluations. The paired analysis covers 42 initializations: the 41 that all three methods completed, and one that failed in all three. On these, Swim2Real lowered AUC40 by 63.4 mm against random directions and by 53.2 mm against permuted directions. Its AUC40 was lower in 34 of the 41 completed pairs against random directions and in 32 against permuted directions. Both comparisons pass Holm correction (p = 1.2 × 10−7 and 7.9 × 10−6).

A second model shows the same effect

With GLM-5.3-Flash in place of Gemini 2.5 Pro, on 24 paired initializations with the same budget and line search, Swim2Real again had lower AUC40 than both controls. Its median AUC40 was 71.1 mm, against 161.1 mm for random and 113.2 mm for permuted directions (Holm-adjusted p = 1.7 × 10−5 and 9.1 × 10−5).

Lower error at frequencies left out of the fit

In an eight-seed study, each method was fitted on five of the eight frequencies and assessed once on the other three. On those three, Swim2Real's error was 53.1 ± 3.8 mm (mean ± SD), against 102.2 ± 64.9 mm for random directions and 65.4 ± 28.5 mm for Bayesian optimization. It had the lower error in 7 of 8 pairs against random directions and in 6 of 8 against Bayesian optimization.

The robot and its simulator

The robot is 0.6 m long and weighs 1.5 kg including electronics. Its compliant tail is built from super-elastic nitinol rods held by five 3D-printed spine segments. Antagonistic tendons, driven by a dual-output crank-slider, bend the tail into an S-shape.

The recordings were made with an overhead 4K camera in a 2 m × 3 m pool. The simulator is a MuJoCo model that splits the tail into five rigid segments joined by hinges and uses a fluid model with five coefficients.

Simulator commands on the real robot

Motor commands generated in simulation also run on the robot. In ten open-loop pool trials, we replayed motor commands from soft actor-critic policies trained with an earlier simulator. The robot's displacement along its initial heading was positive in every trial, 0.38 ± 0.22 m on average.

Code and data

The code (MIT) and the run records (CC BY 4.0) are on GitHub. The records hold every simulator evaluation and VLM reply from 318 calibration runs, and one command recomputes the paper's calibration results from them in a few seconds.
