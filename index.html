Swim2Real: Vision-Language Models for Simulator Calibration of a Soft Robotic Fish

Kevin Qiu1, Kyle Liam Walker2,3, Mike Yan Michelis4, Marek Cygan1, Josie Hughes2

1University of Warsaw 2EPFL 3University of Liverpool 4ETH Zurich

Paper (PDF) arXiv 2603.20827 BibTeX

Swim2Real calibrates a 16-parameter MuJoCo model of a soft robotic fish from swimming videos. A vision-language model compares the real and simulated motion and proposes which fluid, motor and joint parameters to change; a backtracking line search keeps a step only if it lowers marker error.

In an independent, preregistered replication on fifty fresh paired seeds, the proposed directions lowered the mean best-so-far marker error by 63.4 mm against random directions and by 53.2 mm against permuted VLM directions under the same line search and simulator budget, conditional on run completion (Figure 2).

Abstract

Calibrating a soft aquatic robot simulator requires matching coupled deformation and propulsion from limited pool recordings. Swim2Real uses a vision-language model (VLM) to compare real and simulated swimming videos and propose updates to 16 simulator parameters. A backtracking line search tests each direction and accepts updates that reduce marker error. To test whether the proposals carry useful directions, we compare them with random and signed-permuted directions under the same line search and simulator budget. In an independent, preregistered fifty-seed replication, Swim2Real reduces the mean best-so-far error over 40 simulator evaluations by 63.4 mm against random directions and 53.2 mm against permuted directions. Both paired comparisons pass Holm correction (p < 10−5), conditional on run completion. A 24-seed experiment with a second VLM supports the same direction effect. Backtracking averages 16.1 VLM proposals per run versus 39 for Vid2Sid, which attains lower error at the same simulator budget. Ten hardware trials demonstrate open-loop execution of simulator-generated motor commands.

Results

To test whether the proposed directions carry information, we compare them with two matched controls that keep the line search, step sizes and budget and change only the direction. Random directions use no model. Signed-permuted directions query the VLM at their own best parameters so far, then shuffle which parameters receive the proposed changes and assign random signs, keeping the step's length in normalized coordinates before it is clipped to the parameter bounds.

The preregistered replication compares AUC40, the mean best-so-far error over the 40 evaluations, on paired initializations. Swim2Real lowered it by 63.4 mm against random directions and by 53.2 mm against permuted directions, and was lower in 34 of 41 pairs against random and 32 of 41 against permuted directions. Both paired comparisons pass Holm correction (p < 10−5), conditional on run completion: of the fifty seeds, 44 runs completed for Swim2Real, 48 for random and 45 for permuted directions.

A first study on fifteen shared initializations also ran Bayesian optimization, SPSA, two CMA-ES variants, and Vid2Sid, which requests a fresh VLM proposal for every candidate after initialization (our adaptation, with the shared fish prompt and evaluator). These comparisons are descriptive. Vid2Sid reaches lower error at the same simulator budget with 2.4 times as many proposals, so the choice depends on the relative cost of model queries and residual calibration error.

A second model

With GLM-5.3-Flash on 24 paired initializations, using 16 sampled frames per clip and the same budget and line search, the complete method again had lower AUC40 than both controls: a median of 71.1 mm against 161.1 mm for random and 113.2 mm for permuted directions. The paired mean differences were −88.3 mm [−121.2, −58.7] and −58.6 mm [−87.1, −35.0] (unadjusted 95% BCa intervals; Holm p = 1.7 × 10−5 and 9.1 × 10−5). The random-direction runs are reused from the replication, since that control queries no model.

Withheld frequencies

Fitting five command frequencies and assessing the frozen parameters at the other three, Swim2Real reached 53.1 ± 3.8 mm, against 102.2 ± 64.9 mm for random directions and 65.4 ± 28.5 mm for Bayesian optimization, with 7 of 8 and 6 of 8 paired wins. These counts are descriptive, the interval against Bayesian optimization includes zero, and the recordings were seen during development, so this is an interpolation check rather than a test on new data.

Method

A simulator can match one part of a swimming motion while missing another: the tail may bend with the wrong amplitude, or the body may advance too slowly. A scalar trajectory error measures the mismatch but does not say which parameters to change. Swim2Real asks a vision-language model to read the mismatch from video.

Each round (Figure 3), Gemini 2.5 Pro receives simulated and real marker overlays at every fitting frequency, the real video at the worst-matching frequency, per-marker and per-frequency errors, velocities, the current 16 parameters with their bounds, and the outcomes of earlier proposals. It returns a physical diagnosis, new values for the parameters it wants to change, and a confidence. Unknown names are discarded, values are projected to the bounds, and parameters it does not mention stay unchanged. Figure 4 shows the input of the first round.

The proposal is treated as a direction rather than an answer. The line search evaluates the full step, then the half and quarter steps, and accepts the first one that lowers marker error. If none does, the round is rejected and the model is told so in the next prompt. Every evaluation, full or backtracked, counts against the same budget of 40 that every method gets.

In the fifteen-seed study, 97 updates were accepted: 51 at the full step, 29 at the half step and 17 at the quarter step. Backtracking therefore supplied 46 of them (47%), each along a direction whose full step had not improved the incumbent.

One calibration run

Figure 5 replays the run behind Figure 1. Four accepted updates took the error from 311.7 mm to 46.0 mm within ten evaluations. After that, no step of any proposal lowered it, so the parameters stayed where they were. The diagnoses are hypotheses; the marker objective decides which steps are kept.

Robot and data

The robot (Figure 6) is 0.6 m long and weighs 1.5 kg including electronics. Its compliant tail is built from super-elastic nitinol rods held by five 3D-printed spine segments, and antagonistic tendons driven by a dual-output crank-slider bend it into an S-shape.

Nine centerline markers are tracked in overhead 4K video at 59.94 fps in a 2 m × 3 m pool, at eight command labels from 0.50 to 2.25 Hz. The realized tail-beat rates, measured from the video, run from 0.37 to 1.66 Hz. The MuJoCo model splits the tail into five rigid segments joined by hinges and uses a stateless ellipsoid fluid model with five coefficients. One evaluation simulates all eight frequencies in about 13 s on a single core.

Open-loop hardware replay

As a feasibility check, motor commands from soft actor-critic policies were replayed open loop on the robot in ten trials. All ten had positive displacement along the initial heading, +0.382 ± 0.220 m on average, with mean net displacement 0.566 ± 0.344 m. The policies were trained with an earlier simulator, so these trials show command execution, not the effect of the calibration above (Figures 7 and 8).

Limitations

  • The recovered parameters match behavior at eight observed frequencies; they do not uniquely identify material or fluid properties.
  • Seeds vary the optimizer's initialization on the same fish and recordings. New recordings and swimming trajectories are future work.
  • The five-link tail approximation limits continuous bending.
  • The prompt combines video with numerical residuals, so the separate contribution of the video, and whether the model's physical explanations are correct, remain open.
  • The calibration has not been tested on hardware. That needs feedback, measured target attainment, and a matched control.
  • Reused and fresh proposals have not been compared at equal query or wall-clock budgets.
